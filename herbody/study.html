01 · The read

Five findings that change decisions

  1. Free Soul already owns "women's creatine" in the UK. Pure Creatine (June 2025), CreaGlow (creatine + collagen), HydraCreatine, £42m revenue, 268 Boots stores, TikTok Shop's first Super Brand Day of 2026, a rebrand built on 7,000 women's input. "Creatine for her" is no longer a wedge on its own — the complete scoop is. Nobody on amazon.co.uk combines full-dose creatine + collagen + a complete multivitamin + inositol. That square is empty and it is yours to name.
  2. The women's-creatine buyer is older and more cognitive than the pink-pack aesthetic assumes. Industry data puts 76% of social conversation around menopause/perimenopause, the core buyer at 40–65, and motivations ranked cognition > energy > strength; "muscle" is eighth. Women are now 30% of GNC creatine buyers (18% in 2020). Design for a 38-year-old who wants to feel sharp and steady, not a 22-year-old who wants a pump — and you'll capture both.
  3. Under-dosing is the category's open wound. Every women-positioned creatine on Amazon UK sits at 3–3.5g (Warrior, Myprotein 3-in-1, Free Soul HydraCreatine); the clinical and UK-legal-claim threshold is 3g, and the "5g" line is uncontested. Scoop and serving honesty is the #2 complaint in reviews. A true 5g scoop and a real 30 servings is both your best claim and your best trust signal.
  4. Pink is the category, not the differentiator. Wellgard, VitaSense, Bloom, Warrior's pink accents, Myprotein's pastels — on the "creatine for women" grid a pink pouch disappears. The evidence says saturated, hue-congruent colour signals potency and taste, pale pastels signal "healthy but weak", and dark packs out-thumb pastels on Amazon's white. Deep raspberry, not blush.
  5. Sweetness is the #1 flavoured-combo complaint. "Disgustingly sweet", "extremely sweet at 300ml". A light, low-sweetener strawberry-raspberry is a formulation answer to a review problem — make sure Black Label's taste development lands there, and say "not sickly-sweet" in the listing.

02 · The buyer

Who she is and how she decides in two seconds

Two women buy this product and the pack has to talk to both without splitting the difference into mush.

The core buyer · 35–55 · "Steady & sharp"

Perimenopause-aware, strength-training on advice, reads about creatine for brain and mood, hates being marketed to like a teenager. Anxiety she won't say aloud: losing the edge — energy, memory, shape. She trusts experts (Stacy Sims, Mary Claire Haver, Dr Louise Newson in the UK), podcasts and other women her age. Price-tolerant if the dose is honest.

The growth buyer · 24–34 · "Sorted"

Gym-curious, TikTok-fed, already buys collagen and a multi, tired of five tubs. Anxiety: bloat, breakouts, "am I doing this right". Trusts creators and reviews; converts on taste, aesthetics and per-day price. Wants the pouch to look good on the counter.

The purchase drivers this product activates

  • Ritual identity — "one scoop, done" is the product's moat. The pack should feel like a morning object, not a gym accessory. Name the ritual on pack and in the insert ("your daily scoop").
  • Anxiety reduction, obliquely — validate ("you're not imagining the 3pm crash"), normalise, then solve. Never shame, never "anti-ageing". Legally in the UK you carry this through the B-vitamin "reduction of tiredness and fatigue" claim, not through promises.
  • Expertise translated — post-Ordinary consumers want doses on the front and the science explained like a smart friend. Dose transparency is also, per the evidence, the single strongest label-trust lever.
  • Social permission — she needs to see women like her using it. Vine reviews, then a "Designed with her" panel (Free Soul's play), then creator UGC.
  • Sensory anticipation — powders are bought on imagined taste. Real fruit on pack and a "light, not sickly" cue lift both taste and healthiness perception when hue matches the ingredient.

The decision journey and where the pouch enters

Trigger: a podcast, a friend, a GP's offhand "creatine is good for women now". Search: "creatine for women", "is creatine safe for women" (22,500 safety searches a month), then Amazon. On the results grid she scans colour, shape and the biggest word in under two seconds — text is skipped under time pressure. She opens 3–4 listings, compares price per serving, dose, rating count and taste comments, then buys the one that resolves "will it work" and "will I like drinking it" fastest. Post-purchase she validates through the first-week taste and the week-3 feeling — which is why the insert card matters for reviews.

05 · The pouch

Design direction, decided — and a pre-print audit for your current artwork

Your artwork is finalised but unprinted; the honest move is to score it against this before the pouch quote is accepted. The direction below is built from the evidence in §08 and the grid in §03. Where a recommendation rests on weak evidence, it says so.

Layer 1 · Hierarchy (reading order in 0.5–2 seconds)

  1. What it is — the largest type: a category line she is searching for. Recommended: THE COMPLETE DAILY with the sub-line creatine · collagen · multivitamin · inositol. Under time pressure shoppers use one or two visual elements and skip text (Silayoi & Speece) — this line and the colour do the work.
  2. Who makes it — herbody wordmark, second tier, top-left. Short names read healthier (Motoki 2023); you have a good one.
  3. The hook — a single contrasting badge: 5g creatine per scoop. Specific, verifiable wording is the strongest label-trust lever (Atkinson & Rosenthal). Confirm the dose with Black Label before this goes to print.
  4. Proof — 30 servings · 1 scoop a day, tabular numerals, engineered feel.
  5. Everything else — flavour (Strawberry & Raspberry), "food supplement", net weight, mandatory statements at ≥6pt.

Layer 2 · Grid

Type-First Editorial with a single real-fruit accent, not a hero photo. Image-led powders make the fruit the hero of the category, not the brand; a type-led pouch is what Bare Performance and Innermost use to stand apart from image-heavy Bloom clones. Allocation: top third — wordmark + category line; middle — hook badge and the ingredient sub-line with a small hue-matched berry accent or optional powder window; bottom third — servings, flavour, mandatory copy. Keep the seal zones and gusset fold clear.

Layer 3 · Colour system

Round-2 decision: after the first concept round the chosen direction is an alabaster face inside a black-cherry frame (see §07). The berry-ground anchor below remains the bolder alternative and is requested as the one flipped variant. Both obey the same rule: dark, saturated, flavour-congruent contrast on white; no pastel ground.

The evidence, not folklore: saturation signals potency and taste; pale, low-saturation packs read "healthy but weak"; saturation only helps when the hue matches the hero ingredient; dark packs out-thumb pastels on Amazon's white; feminine cues help even healthy products when they're present — but pink pastel is now the category uniform on the women's-creatine grid. So: a saturated, dark berry anchor that is literally the colour of your flavour, feminine by hue rather than by pastel, with cream type for contrast and a bright raspberry accent for the hook.

Layer 4 · Typography

  • Display (category line): a confident contemporary grotesk with personality — Bricolage Grotesque (free, used on this page), or PP Right Grotesk / Söhne Breit if licensing. Set tight, heavy weight, wide enough to survive flexo. The evidence favours moderately bold letters at small sizes and penalises hairline and ultra-bold extremes (Beier & Oderkerk).
  • Wordmark: lowercase herbody in the same family or a softened custom cut — feminine cue through rounded terminals, not script. Script and pastel are the "feminine" defaults the evidence says work but the grid says are saturated.
  • Body: Instrument Sans or Inter at semibold minimum on flexo; 6pt floor for mandatory copy.
  • Numerals: tabular, semibold, given their own weight. The "5g" and "30" are trust objects.

Layer 5 · Claims and badges (maximum four)

  1. 5g creatine per scoop — the hook badge (only if the formula delivers it).
  2. Collagen + vitamin C — small lock-up; the legal collagen story in the UK is "vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of skin".
  3. 30 servings — serving honesty is a live review complaint; make it prominent and true.
  4. One third-party mark — Informed Sport if you can (over 40% of UK/US buyers look for a mark), else "independently lab-tested" with the COA behind it. No "premium quality", no "doctor formulated" without a name. There is no experiment on badge counts, but scepticism rises with label density (Fenko et al.) — group everything into one strip.

Material and finish: matte soft-touch laminate. Gloss reads greasy and less healthy; matte raises perceived naturalness and, for goal-attainment products like yours, perceived effectiveness (Huang et al. 2023). Optional: a 25%-area powder window — evidence says windows lift attractiveness and purchase intent, and gains plateau above 25% exposure; the pink powder against berry film is a genuine differentiator if your converter offers it.

06 · Names & claims

A product name she can say on camera, and blend names that are legal in Britain

Category naming conventions right now: Crea- prefixes (CreaGlow, Creatonics, Creapure), Her- prefixes (Hertime, Hergreens), -Glow suffixes, "+" pairings (Hair + Energy), umlauts (Gruns), and the definite article ("The Health Protein"). Avoid Crea-, Her- and -Glow; they are the crowded lanes.

Product name — the thing on the front and in the title

  • herbody Complete — "The Complete Daily" as category line, "Complete" as the sayable product name. Descriptive of the all-in-one truth, uncontested on Amazon UK, and it is the enemy-framing of every single-ingredient competitor in one word. Recommended.
  • herbody One — "one scoop, one ritual". Short, clean, slightly generic; strong if you plan a range (One, Sleep, Glow) later.
  • herbody Daily Five — names the five pillars (creatine, collagen, vitamins, inositol, botanicals). Proof-furniture built into the name, like Bloom's "38 ingredients".

Trademarked blend names — with the UK rule that governs them

In GB, a brand or blend name that implies a health benefit is itself treated as a health claim under assimilated Regulation 1924/2006 and must be accompanied by an authorised claim. So outcome-led names work only where you have an authorised claim to sit beside them; elsewhere use descriptive or process-led names. Note also that no experiment has tested whether a ™ symbol raises perceived quality — treat the "invention heuristic" as folklore; the real value is un-copyable keywords and content modules.

Non-negotiable

Words that cannot appear on the pouch or listing

  • "Diuretic", "water retention", "de-bloat", "reduces bloating" — unauthorised health claims; "diuretic" risks the product being treated as a medicine by the MHRA.
  • "Balances hormones", "supports PCOS", "menopause relief" — inositol has no authorised claim and these are medicinal. Life-stage language (Wild Nutrition's "45+") is fine; symptom promises are not.
  • "Clinically proven" — needs a trial on the finished product; the "clinically studied" ingredient framing is the honest version.
  • Any collagen benefit stated of collagen itself — route every skin/hair/nail benefit through vitamin C, biotin or zinc's authorised claims.

08 · Evidence

What the design rests on

A = replicated peer-reviewed · B = single study or industry data · C = folklore or unverified. Every citation was checked live; anything unverifiable is flagged rather than quoted.

  • Pale/low-saturation packs read healthier but less tasty; saturated colour reads tastier — Mead & Richerson (2018) JBR; Tijssen et al. (2017) FQP; Kunz et al. (2020) P&M A
  • Saturation only helps when hue matches the hero ingredient — Steiner, Kunz & Florack (2025) BFJ B
  • Saturation signals potency/efficacy (7 studies, n=2,745) — Labrecque et al. (2025) J. Marketing B
  • Colour→health via three mechanisms, 20-study review — Steiner & Florack (2023) Foods A; fluency mediates colour–category fit — Su & Wang (2024) Front. Nutr. B
  • Generic colour psychology ("blue = trust") is weak and context-bound — Elliot & Maier (2012, 2014); Lehmann et al. (2018) meta-analysis Evol. Psych. C
  • Under time pressure, graphics/colour/shape drive choice; text skipped — Silayoi & Speece (2004) BFJ; shelf eye-tracking — Clement et al. (2013) JRCS B
  • Moderately bold letters win at small sizes; extremes lose — Beier & Oderkerk (2019) Acta Psychologica B
  • Specific, verifiable claim wording raises label trust — Atkinson & Rosenthal (2014) J. Advertising B; more labels → complexity and scepticism — Fenko et al. (2016) FQP B
  • Structure/function wording inflates perceived disease benefit — Assadourian et al. (2025) JAMA Netw. Open B
  • >40% of UK/US supplement buyers look for a certification mark — Informed Sport / CIL survey (2026) source B
  • Benefit-suggestive names lock recall to one benefit — Keller, Heckler & Houston (1998) J. Marketing A; fluent names → safer/purer/purchase — Song & Schwarz (2009); Cho (2018); shorter names → healthier — Motoki et al. (2023) P&M B
  • ™/® "invention heuristic" — no empirical study found C
  • Feminine-cued packs raise attractiveness and purchase intent for healthy products — Zhu et al. (2015) Social Psychology B; "pink it and shrink it" backlash — Vitafoods 2022, Nutritional Outlook 2026 C
  • Transparent windows raise purchase intent; gains plateau at ~25% exposure — Simmonds et al. (2018) FQP; Calderón et al. (2026) FRI B
  • Matte = natural/effective for approach products; gloss = greasy, less healthy — Marckhgott & Kamleitner (2019); Ye et al. (2020) JCP; Huang et al. (2023) P&M A
  • Women's-creatine buyer data (76% menopause discussion, 40–65 core, cognition first) — NutraIngredients 2026; women 30% of GNC creatine buyers — MedicalXpress; "creatine for women" ~110k searches/mo, +123% YoY — Rising Trends, Bupa 2026 B
